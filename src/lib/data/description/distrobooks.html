Distrobooks is bookkeeping for distributors who work on consignment. Stock comes in from suppliers, goes out to shops before anyone has paid for it, and the money arrives later, a bit at a time.

It's a subscription SaaS. Each business signs up as its own organisation and gets inventory, suppliers and buyers, purchase orders, sales invoices, returns, expenses, a ledger, reports, and a public stock menu it can share. The frontend is SvelteKit with Tailwind. The backend is Flask with SQLAlchemy on MySQL, with Stripe for billing and DigitalOcean Spaces for media. I joined after the first version and stayed on it for well over a year, so most of what's here is features I added to a product that people were already using.

In consignment the hard question is never "what did we sell?" It's "who still owes us for what?"

Stock that belongs to someone else

Stock enters the system through a supplier's purchase order, which creates or tops up inventory and records what the business owes that supplier. It leaves through a sales invoice to a buyer, and that invoice can be marked as consignment. In that case the goods are out of the warehouse but not yet sold, and they show up on their own consignment screen listing which buyer holds what and how much of it is unpaid. Payments can be recorded against an invoice in parts, and the status moves between unpaid, partly paid, and paid as they come in.

Returns were where the maths kept going wrong. I added return invoices on both sides: goods going back to a supplier, and goods coming back from a buyer. Each one had to adjust stock, what is owed, and the ledger together. The "total owed" figures had to be rewritten to take returns into account, and the ledger now shows purchases, sales, and returns in one list that can be filtered by date and exported to CSV for the accountant.

Every change leaves a trail

Because several people in one business edit the same numbers, every inventory item, account, sale, and purchase order has an audit table next to it. Each change saves a full copy of the row along with the type of change, the time, and the user who made it. The app shows that history on the item's own page. When a stock count looks wrong, someone can see exactly which invoice or edit changed it. A good part of my work was tracking down audit mismatches, where the history and the current value no longer added up.

Menus and QR codes

Distributors wanted to show shops what was available without giving them a login. Each organisation gets a public inventory menu at its own address, grouped by category, with live stock levels, photos, and short product videos. The server can also draw the whole menu as an image with Pillow, so it can be sent in a message or printed. Inventory items have QR codes, and when building an invoice you can add items by scanning them with the device camera instead of searching a list.

Trials, plans, and Stripe

I built the subscription system. Plans are Stripe products, and their limits, like the number of accounts or invoices, are stored in the product's metadata, so changing a plan doesn't need a code change. New organisations get a fourteen-day trial with a small cap on accounts and invoices. When the trial ends they're sent to an expired page until they subscribe. Checkout, upgrades, cancellations, and renewals all go through Stripe, and webhooks keep the subscription state in the app in step with Stripe's. Features such as the in-app assistant chat are turned on or off depending on the plan.

At a glance

  • — Multi-tenant SaaS with roles per organisation
  • — Consignment tracking with partial payments
  • — Purchase orders, sales, returns on both sides
  • — Full audit history for every record
  • — Ledger and reports with CSV export and P&L
  • — Public stock menu, also drawn as a shareable image
  • — QR scanning to add items to invoices
  • — Stripe subscriptions with trials, limits, and webhooks

Working on a live financial product taught me to change things carefully. A migration that rewrites how money is added up has to be correct on old data as well as new, and the audit trail is usually what proves whether it was.
